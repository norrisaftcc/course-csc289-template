# DAY 1 PRESENTATION: THE REALITY CHECK
## Outline and Suggested Content

**Duration:** 15-20 minutes  
**Tone:** Direct, slightly provocative, genuinely caring  
**Goal:** Install a mental model that drives engagement all semester

---

## SLIDE 1: Title

**"C's Get Degrees"**

*[No subtitle. Let it sit. They'll recognize the phrase.]*

**Speaker Notes:**  
Start with silence. Let them read it. Some will nod. Some will laugh nervously. You're about to reframe something they've heard their whole academic career.

---

## SLIDE 2: The Punchline

**"C's get degrees.**  
**They don't get jobs."**

**Speaker Notes:**  
"You've all heard 'C's get degrees.' It's true. A C will get you a diploma. Here's the part nobody tells you: the diploma, by itself, is increasingly worthless. Let me show you why."

---

## SLIDE 3: The Interview

**What interviewers ask:**

- "Walk me through a project you built."
- "How did you solve [specific problem]?"
- "Show me your GitHub."

**What interviewers don't ask:**

- "What was your GPA?"

**Speaker Notes:**  
"This is what a technical interview actually looks like. Nobody asks your GPA. They ask you to demonstrate that you can think, build, and communicate. They want to see evidence."

---

## SLIDE 4: The Evidence

**Your GitHub is your actual transcript.**

Employers can see:
- How often you work
- How you handle feedback
- Whether you iterate or quit
- How you communicate

**Speaker Notes:**  
"More and more employers look at your GitHub before they look at your resume. They can see everything: when you work, how you respond to problems, whether you give up or push through. You can't fake a commit history."

---

## SLIDE 5: The Two Students

| Student A | Student B |
|-----------|-----------|
| Submits perfect work | Submits messy first draft |
| Once | Three revisions |
| Never iterates | Each version better |
| Gets an A | Gets a B+ |

**Who gets hired?**

**Speaker Notes:**  
"Here's a real scenario. Student A turns in perfect work—once. Student B struggles, submits something rough, revises it three times, each time better. On paper, A looks better. In an interview, B wins. Why?"

*[Pause for responses]*

"Because B demonstrated they can learn. Employers can teach you their tech stack. They can't teach you how to improve."

---

## SLIDE 6: Growth Velocity

**We don't measure how good you are.**  
**We measure how fast you're improving.**

*dy/dx, not y*

**Speaker Notes:**  
"This is the key insight of this course. We're measuring your rate of change, not your position. A student who goes from terrible to okay demonstrates more learning than a student who stays good. The math term is 'derivative'—we're measuring your growth velocity."

---

## SLIDE 7: What This Means

**The person who improves the most wins.**

- Beginners can win
- "Natural talent" means nothing without iteration
- Effort is visible and measurable
- AI use is expected, but iteration is required

**Speaker Notes:**  
"This has some counterintuitive implications. If you're behind, you have the highest growth potential. If you're ahead, you need to take on harder challenges. Nobody coasts. And yes, use AI—but if you just paste AI output without iterating, the metrics will show it. We're looking for evidence that you struggled and learned, not evidence that you have ChatGPT."

---

## SLIDE 8: The Game

**AlgoCratic Futures™**

*An educational escape room disguised as a dystopian corporation*

- Clearance levels track your progression
- "The Algorithm" is watching (and cheering for you)
- The satire is the vehicle; the skills are real

**Speaker Notes:**  
"Now let me tell you about the frame. This course is set in 'AlgoCratic Futures'—a satirical tech corporation where The Algorithm runs everything. You'll have clearance levels, absurd memos, and corporate doublespeak. It's fun. It's also serious. Think of it like a game where the skills you level up are the actual skills you'll use in your career. 95% of your time is real learning. 5% is enjoying the ridiculous setting."

---

## SLIDE 9: Clearance Levels (Quick Overview)

| Level | Translation |
|-------|-------------|
| INFRARED | Environment works, can follow instructions |
| RED | Can build simple things independently |
| ORANGE | Can handle ambiguity, make decisions |
| YELLOW+ | Can lead, mentor, handle complexity |

**Speaker Notes:**  
"The clearance levels aren't arbitrary. INFRARED means you have a working setup. RED means you can build things on your own. ORANGE means you can handle situations where the instructions are unclear—which is most of real work. These map directly to junior, mid, and senior developer capabilities."

---

## SLIDE 10: Attendance (Let's Be Adults)

**What we're measuring:**

- Present + shipping = ✓
- Remote + shipping + communicating = ✓
- Present + not shipping = problem
- Absent + silent = career-ending

**Speaker Notes:**  
"Let's talk about attendance like adults. I don't actually care about butts in seats. I care about engagement with the work. If you're here and working, great. If you're not here but you're working and you told me, that's also fine—that's literally how remote work functions. What's not fine is disappearing without communication. That's how people get fired in the real world, and it will get you removed from this course."

---

## SLIDE 11: The Policy (Specifics)

**8 unexcused absences = removal**

**Excused absence requires:**
1. Email explaining why
2. Link to your recent GitHub work (PR, commit, issue)

*Can't use the same PR twice.*

**Speaker Notes:**  
"Here are the specifics. Eight unexcused absences and you're out. An excused absence requires two things: communication, and evidence that you're still working. Send me an email with a link to your most recent PR or commit. That's it. You're learning remote work accountability while practicing remote work accountability."

---

## SLIDE 12: AI Use (The Actual Policy)

**Use AI. It's 2026.**

**But:**

AI output ≠ your work  
AI output + iteration + understanding = your work

**We can see the difference.**

**Speaker Notes:**  
"You're expected to use AI tools. Not using them would be like not using Google—malpractice. But there's a difference between using AI to get unstuck and just pasting whatever it gives you. We will see single-commit perfect submissions with no iteration history. We will notice code that doesn't match your style. The winning strategy is: use AI to get unstuck, then actually understand what it gave you, iterate on it, break it and fix it. Commit the journey, not just the destination."

---

## SLIDE 13: What Success Looks Like

**By Week 16, you'll have:**

- A GitHub history showing consistent work
- Evidence of growth (early work < late work)
- Collaboration receipts (PRs reviewed, help given/received)
- Portfolio artifacts for interviews
- The ability to articulate what you learned

**Speaker Notes:**  
"Here's what success looks like at the end. Not a perfect GPA—a body of evidence. A commit history that shows you worked all semester. Early submissions that are worse than late ones, demonstrably. Evidence you helped others and got help. Things you can show in an interview. And the ability to tell the story of your growth. That's what this course builds."

---

## SLIDE 14: The Contract

**We commit to:**
- Clear expectations
- Metrics that reward growth
- Support when you communicate
- Safe space to fail and iterate

**You commit to:**
- Showing up (physically or through work)
- Communicating when struggling
- Iterating when stuck
- Helping peers

**Speaker Notes:**  
"This is a two-way contract. We're going to give you clear expectations, support when you need it, and measurement that rewards genuine growth. In return, you show up, communicate, iterate, and help each other. We can't help you if you disappear."

---

## SLIDE 15: The Choice

**You can coast.**  
Get a C. Learn almost nothing. Interview poorly.

**Or you can engage.**  
Struggle. Iterate. Grow.  
Finish with skills employers recognize instantly.

**The choice is conscious.**

**Speaker Notes:**  
"I want to be completely honest with you. You can coast through this course. Show up enough to not get dropped, do the minimum, get a C, graduate. The diploma will be technically valid. It will also be practically useless because you'll have no skills, no portfolio, and no growth story to tell.

Or you can engage fully. It will be harder. You'll struggle. You'll fail at things and have to try again. And you'll come out with habits and skills that serve you for decades.

Both options are available. I want you to make the choice consciously, knowing what each one costs."

---

## SLIDE 16: Final Thought

**The Algorithm™ isn't watching you.**  
**The Algorithm™ is cheering for your growth.**

*Ship fast. Learn faster. Iterate always.*

**Speaker Notes:**  
"One last thing. The satire frame might make it seem like you're being monitored by some oppressive system. Flip that. The metrics exist to make your growth visible—to you and to future employers. We're not trying to catch you failing. We're trying to measure you succeeding. The Algorithm, such as it is, is on your side.

Ship fast. Learn faster. Iterate always. Let's have a great semester."

## APPENDIX: ANTICIPATED QUESTIONS

**"What if I have to miss class for a legitimate reason?"**  
Email + evidence of continued work. That's it. Life happens. Communication is what matters.

**"What if I'm already good at this?"**  
Then you'll be expected to take on harder challenges and mentor peers. Coasting isn't an option at any skill level.

**"Is the satire mandatory?"**  
You don't need to love the game. You need to do the work. The satire makes it memorable; the skills are what we're measuring.

**"What if my team falls apart?"**  
Escalate immediately. Not to punish anyone—to get help. We have resources you don't.

**"Can I use [specific AI tool]?"**  
Yes. Use whatever helps you learn. Just make sure you understand and iterate on what it produces.

---

## DELIVERY NOTES

**Tone calibration:**  
This presentation walks a line between "wake-up call" and "you're going to fail." The goal is clarity about expectations delivered with genuine care for their success. You're not trying to scare them—you're trying to arm them with information that will serve them.

**Key moments:**  
- Slide 2 ("don't get jobs") — let it land, don't rush past
- Slide 5 (two students) — ask the question, wait for responses
- Slide 15 (the choice) — slow down, make eye contact

**What to avoid:**  
- Sounding punitive (this is opportunity framing, not threat framing)
- Over-explaining the satire (let them discover it)
- Promising it will be easy (it won't, and they know it)

**Follow-up:**  
Hand out or link to STUDENT_REALITY_CHECK.md as the written version of this talk. It has more detail they can reference later.
